Hi everyone, we're Group 5. Today we compare three TCP congestion control algorithms: BBRv1, CUBIC, and Reno. Everything ran on the real Linux TCP stack, over one emulated network path. We collected the data on 23 November.

Research problem

Why loss and queue depth matter

A busy link can still give one flow very little capacity or impose long delays.

RQ1

Single-flow loss response

How do goodput, retransmissions, and excess RTT change for BBRv1, CUBIC, and Reno as random loss rises?

RQ2 and RQ3

Sharing a bottleneck

How does queue size affect BBRv1–CUBIC sharing, aggregate goodput, retransmissions, and delay?

Scope · One emulated path · Bulk TCP · Drop-tail queue

II2202 · Group 502 / 09
So why look at this? A link can be fully used and still treat flows very differently. We asked two kinds of questions. First, one flow on its own: what happens when random loss goes up? Second, two flows sharing a bottleneck: does the queue size change who gets the bandwidth, and what does it cost in delay? The path stays the same throughout, 100 Mb/s with a 50 ms round trip. Our expectations going in were simple. At 2% loss, BBRv1 should keep at least 90% of its goodput, and CUBIC and Reno less than half. With two flows, BBRv1's share should drop as the queue gets deeper. The deepest queue should add the most delay. The shallowest should cause the most retransmissions.

Background

How the senders respond to loss

Reno

Increases its window by about one segment per RTT. Roughly halves it on loss.

RFC 5681

CUBIC

Grows its window with a cubic function of time. Keeps about 70% on loss.

RFC 9438

BBRv1

Estimates delivery bandwidth and minimum RTT. Uses them to pace and limit inflight data.

Cardwell et al. (2017)

Prior experiments found that buffer depth changes the BBRv1–CUBIC split Jaeger et al. (2019)

II2202 · Group 503 / 09
A bit of background. Reno and CUBIC both treat loss as the signal to slow down. Reno roughly halves its window. CUBIC backs off less and grows back faster. The Mathis model gives a rough prediction for Reno: throughput falls with the square root of the loss rate. And when loss is frequent, CUBIC ends up behaving a lot like Reno. BBRv1 is different. It builds a model of bandwidth and round-trip time instead. It still has to resend lost packets, though. Earlier experiments found that buffer size changes how BBR and CUBIC share a link. That's why we vary the queue.

Here's the testbed. Three network namespaces on one machine. The router is the bottleneck: a 100 Mb/s token bucket with a plain drop-tail queue. The 50 ms delay sits on the ACK path. In Part A, a separate netem on the sender drops packets at random. Part B has two flows, and the second one starts five seconds after the first. For each queue size, BBR went first in three runs and CUBIC in two. Every condition got five runs, in shuffled order. One caveat: we ran this on WSL2, not on bare metal as we first planned.

A word on data quality, because our first check failed. At 0.5% loss we only measured 0.078%. Once we capped GSO to one segment, it read 0.494%. After that fix, every validation check passed. One run hit 100% CPU, so we ran it again. For the analysis, we skip the start-up and use a fixed window. In Part B, time counts from the first flow's start, and the window ends at 300 seconds. To check that sharing had settled, we compared the first and last 30-second blocks. We also redid the key numbers over just the last 120 seconds. Each run counts once. The medians and ranges you'll see are over those five runs.

II2202 · Group 506 / 09
First result, one flow at a time. Note the log scale on the y-axis. BBRv1 barely moves. At 2% loss it still keeps 96% of its goodput. CUBIC and Reno fall fast. At 2% they keep under 2% of their baseline, 1.7 and 1.9 percent. The dotted line is the Mathis prediction, using the constant for delayed ACKs. Both sit a little above it. One thing we got wrong: we expected the gap between CUBIC and Reno to shrink at high loss. It grew instead, from 0.10 to 0.17 Mb/s. Small, but the wrong direction. And without injected loss, BBRv1 had the lowest delay: 12.3 ms of P95 excess RTT, against about 48 for CUBIC and 47 for Reno. At 2% loss, all three resent about 19 or 20 segments per thousand.

Now two flows, one BBRv1 and one CUBIC. On the left is BBR's share of the goodput. The dashed line is an even split. With a shallow queue, a quarter of a BDP, BBR takes almost everything, about 97%. At 1 BDP it still has 85%. At 4 BDP it flips. BBR gets 43.5%, so CUBIC now has the bigger part. The right chart shows the price. Going from 0.25 to 4 BDP adds about 182 ms to the P95 delay. Retransmissions peak in the shallow queue: about 6 per thousand for BBR, 14 for CUBIC. All 15 runs passed our stability check, and the pattern holds over the last 120 seconds as well.

Conclusion and future work

Aggregate goodput barely moved; sharing and delay did

What the experiment showed

  • BBRv1 kept most of its goodput at 2% random loss. CUBIC and Reno each retained below 2%.
  • At 0.25 BDP, BBRv1 dominated the split and both flows retransmitted most often.
  • At 4 BDP, sharing was more even and P95 excess RTT was 181.7 ms higher than at 0.25 BDP.

Next experiments

  • Test intermediate queue sizes with longer overlaps and balanced starts.
  • Compare a newer BBR version and active queue management on bare-metal Linux.

Scope · BBRv1 · Equal 50 ms base RTTs · One 100 Mb/s drop-tail bottleneck

II2202 · Group 508 / 09
To wrap up. Within the range we tested, the picture is quite clear. BBRv1 held up under random loss. CUBIC and Reno did not. In a shared bottleneck, queue size decided who got the bandwidth. And total throughput stayed high the whole time, even when the split was very unequal. So throughput alone would have hidden all of this. The deepest queue gave the fairest split, but also the longest delay. For our hypotheses: the retention part of H1 held, H2 held at both ends of the range, and H3's delay and retransmission patterns held. What we didn't test is loss and queue size together, or exactly where the two shares cross.
